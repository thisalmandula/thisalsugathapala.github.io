Following a successful licentiate defence, copies of my thesis started finding their way to different people working on microplastics, introducing my research and the somewhat different perspective I was bringing to the problem. Little did I know that handing out those copies would eventually open the door to some unexpected opportunities.

One day, I received a surprising email from Agnes Faxén at the Skagerak Facility, University of Gothenburg. Somehow, one of my thesis copies had found its way to her. She had read about my work, *Multi-scale modeling of microplastics transport in marine environments*, and invited me to take part in GUIDE Days 2025, an event bringing together researchers and industry around the topic of microplastics and marine health.

And that was how my GUIDE Days journey began.

## Taking microplastic research aboard R/V Skagerak

I was invited to join the academic team demonstrating how we study microplastics in the marine environment. Luckily for me, I was not walking into a completely unfamiliar group. Several people I already knew through my research collaborations were part of the team, including Martin Hassellöv, Karin Mattsson, and Irina Polovodova .

The first day took place aboard R/V Skagerak, where I was given the microplastic visualization station. My job was essentially to introduce participants from different national authorities and the plastics industry to the problem of microplastic pollution and show them what we actually mean when we talk about "microplastics pollution".

That turned out to be a lot of fun.

Some of the plastic samples were large enough to hold in your hand. Others were so small that we needed a microscope to properly see them. We had a microscope station alongside the samples, which I also helped run, and I spent much of the day explaining what we were looking at, discussing how they enter the marine environment, and what happens to them afterwards.

What made the day particularly interesting was that the learning went both ways. While I was explaining the environmental and scientific side of microplastics, I also had the opportunity to speak directly with people from the plastics industry and learn much more about how the industry actually works. Those conversations gave me a perspective that is difficult to get from reading research papers and running simulations.

The rest of the day followed the journey of how we study microplastics in the ocean. Participants were shown how CTD measurements are taken to characterize the water column, how surface waters can be sampled for floating microplastics, and how sediment cores are collected from the seabed and later analysed.

What I particularly liked was that these were not simply presentations about scientific methods. The measurements and sampling were being demonstrated in real time aboard the research vessel. It connected the entire chain, from observing the marine environment and collecting samples to understanding what those measurements can eventually tell us about microplastic pollution.

## Where modelling enters the picture

The second day moved from the research vessel to presentations and discussions. This time, my role was to show where modelling fits into everything we had seen the previous day.

I presented how numerical models can help us understand the fate of microplastics after they enter the ocean. Observations can tell us where particles are found, while modelling gives us another way of asking how they might have reached those locations, how currents and turbulence transport them, and how processes such as settling and interactions with the surrounding environment influence where they eventually accumulate.

Some of the most interesting discussions for me were about what comes after the science: **how can modelling contribute to policy and decision-making?** Hearing directly from representatives working with policy also gave me a much better picture of how scientific evidence moves through the different stages between research and actual regulation.

That is a question I have become increasingly interested in during my PhD. A model does not have to exist only to produce another scientific paper. If we can understand where microplastics originate, how they are transported, and where they are likely to accumulate, modelling can potentially help inform monitoring strategies, identify areas of concern, evaluate possible interventions, and provide scientific evidence for environmental decision-making.

## Seeing the problem from both sides

GUIDE Days ended up being quite different from a typical academic conference. Instead of mostly explaining research to other researchers working on similar problems, I found myself discussing microplastics with people who encounter the same problem from very different directions.

I went there to explain what we know about microplastic pollution and how we model it, but I came away having learned a great deal myself—particularly about the plastics industry and the questions that arise when scientific understanding has to meet practical decisions.

And all of it started because I handed out a copy of my thesis and, somehow, it reached the right person.
